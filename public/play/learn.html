1What is Fish?

If you've played Go Fish, you already know the heart of it: you ask other players for cards and try to collect complete sets. Fish adds two twists.

  • You play in teams. Six players, two teams of three. Your team wins sets together.
  • You ask for one exact card, like "Coral, give me the Q♠", and you only ask the other team.

Fish is also called Literature or Canadian Fish. No luck with the cards, no betting: the team that pays attention wins.

2The table

You're always at the bottom of the screen. Teams sit alternately around the table: the players on either side of you are opponents, your two teammates are at the top left and top right, and the third opponent sits at the top.

  • In the app, teammates have a blue ring around their picture and opponents a pink one.
  • The number under each player is how many cards they hold. Everyone can see it.
  • Empty seats are filled by bots, so there's always a full table.
  • The strip at the top shows the score (US and THEM) and which of the nine sets are won.

3The cards

Fish uses 54 cards: a normal deck plus two jokers. They're split into nine sets of six. Every suit makes two sets, a Low one (2 to 7) and a High one (9 to Ace). The four 8s and the two jokers make the ninth.

Everyone gets nine cards, usually a mix of several sets. Nobody can see anyone else's cards.

4How to win

  1. Get all six cards of a set onto your team. They can be spread across you and your teammates.
  2. Declare it: say which teammate holds each of the six. Get it right and your team wins the set.
  3. First team to win five sets wins the game. Five is a majority of nine, so there's never a tie.

5Your turn, tap by tap

When it's your turn, YOUR TURN lights up on the table. You'll usually ask for a card:

  1. Tap Ask.
  2. Under Who, pick an opponent.
  3. Under Which card, pick the card you want. The app only shows cards you're allowed to ask for.
  4. Tap Ask [name] for [card].
✅

They have it: the card comes to you, and you ask again.

Keep going as long as you keep getting cards.

❌

They don't: your turn ends, and it becomes their turn.

Missing is normal. Even the best players miss a lot.

The three asking rules

  • Only ask the other team. You can't ask a teammate.
  • Only ask in a set you already have a card of. To ask for the 4♥ you need at least one Low Heart.
  • Never ask for a card you hold.

Say you hold

You can ask for or any High Spade except your 9♠. You can't ask for the 9♥: that's High Hearts, and you hold none of those.

Out of cards?

You're still in the game. When your turn comes, pick a teammate to take it for you. Your team can still win.

6Declaring a set, tap by tap

Do this on your turn, when you're sure your team holds all six cards of a set:

  1. Tap Declare.
  2. Pick the set. Each one shows how many of it you hold.
  3. For each card, tap who has it: You or a teammate. Your own cards are filled in for you.
  4. Tap Declare [set].
Careful

If any card is wrong, the other team wins the set, even if your team really had all six. Only declare when you're sure who has each card. Declaring doesn't end your turn.

9Questions beginners ask

Can I ask my teammate for a card?

No, only the other team. Teammates can't trade; you win by pulling cards away from the opponents.

Why can't I ask for some cards?

You can only ask in a set you hold a card of, and never for a card you hold. The app only shows the cards you're allowed to ask for.

What happens if I declare wrong?

The other team wins that set, even if your team really had all six. When in doubt, wait.

Does it have to be me who declares?

No. Anyone on your team can declare on their own turn.

Where do the cards go after a set is declared?

All six are shown to everyone and leave the game.

I ran out of cards. Am I out?

No. Your team plays on, and on your turn you hand it to a teammate who still has cards.

Why can I only see the last two moves?

Remembering what happened is the skill, just like at a real table where nobody writes things down. Part 2 shows you how to keep track.

What if I don't move in time?

The game makes a move for you. Miss two turns in a row and it keeps playing for you until you tap "I'm back".

Can a game end in a tie?

No. There are nine sets, so one team always reaches five first.

Do I need an account?

No. Guests can play everything. A free account adds a rating, a profile and a spot on the leaderboard.

Your first game: just three things
  1. Ask in the set you hold the most of. It's the one you're closest to finishing.
  2. Watch the asks in your sets: who asked for what, and who got it.
  3. Only declare when you know who has every card. Otherwise, wait.

1Every ask is a clue

This is the whole game. Every ask tells the whole table something, whether it hits or misses.

🗣️

Coral asks Maya for the Q♠.

So Coral holds at least one High Spade, and Coral doesn't have the Q♠ (you can't ask for your own card). Both are true before Maya even answers.

✅

Maya has it. The Q♠ moves to Coral.

Now everyone knows exactly where the Q♠ is, and it stays there until it moves again in front of everyone.

❌

Or Maya doesn't have it. The turn passes to Maya.

Then neither Coral nor Maya has the Q♠. It's with one of the other four players.

Put three facts together and you can often pin a card down:

  1. Who asked in which set. They hold at least one card of it.
  2. Who said no to which card. They don't have it.
  3. Where cards moved. Those locations are certain.

Add hand counts: a player with no cards holds nothing, and a player with one card left can be holding only one of the cards you're wondering about.

What the engine found

Careful bookkeeping is most of the game. An engine that only tracked these facts perfectly won every single one of 400 paired deals against one that played on general hunches. The gap between a casual player and a good one is almost entirely "did you track every clue, and chain them together?"

Try it

Coral asks Jonah for the Q♠. Jonah says no. Which of these do you not know for sure?

2Only two moves on screen

The app shows the last two moves. Everything before that is up to your memory. That's what makes Fish a game of skill.

What's worth remembering, in order

  1. Cards you saw move. A card stays with whoever won it until it moves again, and every move happens in front of you. These are your certainties.
  2. Which sets each player has shown. Anyone who asked in a set holds a card of it.
  3. Who said no to what. These rule cards out.

How to hold it in your head

  • Remember by player, not by move. Not "three moves ago Tide asked Maya…" but "Tide: Low Hearts, has the J♥". One short line per opponent.
  • Start with the sets you hold. You can only ask in those, so they pay off first. Add the others as you get comfortable.
  • Forget declared sets. Once a set is won, all six cards are shown and leave the game. Drop them from your memory.
  • Check against hand counts. If your picture says Reef holds four cards you know of but Reef's seat shows three, something in your picture is wrong.
Try it

Ten moves ago, Tide won the J♥ from Maya. Nobody has asked Tide for it since. Where is the J♥ now?

3Choosing your ask

Keeping the turn is how you win. Every hit gives you another ask, so a long turn can gather a whole set.

Rule 1: ask for the card you're surest of

A card you watched move to someone is a sure thing, so cash it in. Next best: a card only one player can be holding, given who said no and who has shown that set.

Rule 2: if it's close, think about who gets the turn if you miss

A miss hands the turn to the player you asked. A player with lots of cards does more with a turn than one with two. So when two asks look equally likely, ask the opponent with fewer cards.

Rule 3: if it's still close, push the sets you're winning

Go after the sets your team already holds most of. Finishing nearly-complete sets beats spreading your effort around.

What the engine found
  • Rules 2 and 3 each made the engine stronger, and together they helped more than either alone.
  • They only work as tie-breakers. When the engine let them outweigh a clearly better chance of getting the card, it played worse. Rule 1 always comes first.
  • Going after the player who's nearly out of cards did nothing. Their low count matters for rule 2, not as a target.
  • Misses are normal. Even the strongest engine misses about 2 asks in 5. Judge your play by how many cards you win per turn, not by how often you miss.
  • Asks are shakiest at the start and most accurate in the middle of the game, once the clues have piled up. Early asks are partly for information.
  • Showing a new set costs a little. When two asks are close, ask in a set you've already shown.
Try it

You hold the 9♦ and K♦. The A♦ is either with Tide (2 cards) or Reef (7 cards), and it's a coin flip which. Who do you ask?

4Declaring without blowing it

A wrong declaration gives the set to the other team. In a race to five, that's a two-set swing.

What the engine found

95% of the engine's wrong declarations were about its own team: all six cards were on the team, and it named the wrong teammate for one of them. An opponent still holding a card almost never happened. The danger isn't "do we have them all?" but "who has which?"

Count the cards nobody has ever asked for

A card that moved in front of you, or that a teammate asked for, has a known place. A card that was dealt to a teammate and has never come up could be with either of them. Each of those is a guess. That count, not how many of the set you hold, is your real risk.

Holding five with the sixth never seen feels safe, but it's a coin flip between your two teammates. Holding one while the other five all moved in front of you is completely safe.

Once your team holds all six, there's no hurry

An opponent can only ask in a set they hold a card of, so nobody can take these cards from you. The cards in that set will never move again. Hand counts still help, though: a teammate who runs out of cards holds none of the set, and that can settle the guess.

But once you know, declare right away

Strong players declare the moment they're sure. There's nothing to gain by sitting on a set you know, and when your team is on four, declaring wins the game.

What the engine found

An engine that declared when it was "probably right" (60–70% sure) lost clearly to one that waited until it actually knew. Waiting until it was certain made no difference compared with waiting until it was very nearly sure, because that in-between case hardly ever came up. You either know the split or you don't. If you have to guess, wait.

Try it

Your team holds all of Low Clubs. Which declaration is riskier?

5Playing as a team, without talking

There's no chat, so your asks do the talking.

  • Your asks speak for you. Asking in a set tells your teammates you hold a card of it, so they'll place cards with you when they declare.
  • Read your teammates the same way. What they ask for tells you which sets they hold, and every card they win has a known place. That's exactly what you need to declare correctly.
  • Anyone on the team can declare, on their own turn. If a teammate is better placed to know the split, leave it to them.
  • Out of cards? Pass to the teammate most likely to keep a long turn going, usually the one with more cards and clearer clues.

6Beating the clock

  • Public tables give you 20 seconds a move. Private tables use whatever the host sets.
  • Plan during other people's turns. By the time it's your turn, know your first ask and your backup.
  • If time runs out, the game plays a move for you. Miss two turns in a row and it keeps playing for you until you tap "I'm back".
  • Learn without the clock first. Against the bots, Settings → Time per move → No limit.

7Myths the engine busted

"Good cards win games." Ten thousand deals were each played twice, with the teams swapping hands. The deal explained none of the results. Fish has no high cards: every set is worth one, and cards keep moving.
"Going first is an advantage." No measurable advantage for any seat.
"The 8s and Jokers set plays differently." It's won as often and as late as any other set. Treat it normally.
"Finish off the player with one card left." No effect. What matters is not handing the turn to an opponent with lots of cards.
"One game shows how well I played." In a single game, luck in which asks happen to land outweighs skill. Your skill shows over many games.

8Your practice plan

  1. Watch one game. The bots play every seat, yours included. Follow one set from the deal to its declaration.
  2. Play the bots with no clock. Track just the sets you hold. Before each ask, say to yourself why that card should be there.
  3. Add one opponent. Keep a one-line note in your head for them: the sets they've shown and the cards they've won.
  4. Turn the clock on. 30 seconds, then 20, the speed of public tables.
  5. Play people. A private table with friends, then Quick Play for a rating.
